## ABSTRACT
When we compare a DNA mixture profile to a single person of interest, there are often just two competing ex-
planations considered, and the comparison of how likely these are to lead to the observed mixture is summarized
by a likelihood ratio. However, in more complex cases this does not suffice, e.g., when there are multiple persons
of interest. One can then compute several likelihood ratios, corresponding to several pairs of hypotheses, and
subsequently decide which one(s) to report. This may lead to the computation of a rather large number of such
likelihood ratios. In this article we advocate a systematic approach that starts by describing all relevant hy-
potheses. For each hypothesis, we then compute its likelihood (i.e., the probability to see the genetic data if the
hypothesis is true). Based on the likelihoods of all considered hypotheses, one can then make a summary of the
findings to report. This may be on the level of the considered hypotheses and/or with likelihood ratios per person
of interest. We illustrate with several examples how this approach assists interpretation. The likelihoods sum-
marize how the trace can help to distinguish between the considered hypotheses, in the sense that they transform
the prior odds on them into posterior odds, without having to assign prior probabilities on the hypotheses for the
calculation of the likelihoods themselves. On the other hand likelihood ratios (LR’s) for individual PoI’s cannot
be  obtained without these priors. In  many cases these LR’s  will be  quite insensitive to  the  choice of  prior
probabilities but in other cases they will be; we give examples of both.We argue that the table of likelihoods of
the considered hypotheses is a more natural analog of the LR provided in the simple case with one PoI and two
considered hypotheses, compared to the computation of a LR per PoI. We end with a discussion of the choice of
prior probabilities, of the existing recommendations for this situation, and on reporting.
## 1. Introduction
In line with many recommendations (e.g. [1]) it is standard practice
to summarize the evidential information pointing towards contribution
of a person of interest to a DNA trace in the form of a likelihood ratio
(LR). A  LR  expresses which of  two considered hypotheses is  best at
explaining the  observed evidential profile, and  how much better its
explanation is compared to the explanation of its alternative. In many
situations there is only one person of interest and the hypotheses to
consider are generic, assuming all contributors to the sample are unre-
lated to each other, and that the person of interest contributed to the
sample under hypothesis H
## 1
, versus did not contribute under the alter-
native hypothesis H
## 2
.  In  that case, one  computes (and reports) the
likelihood ratio with respect to these two hypotheses. This LR enables
one to obtain posterior odds on these hypotheses provided one has prior
odds for them.
The analysis becomes more involved when there are multiple per-
sons of interest (PoI). There are then more than two possibilities for who
contributed. For example, with two PoI we have (at least) four possi-
bilities of interest: that they both contributed, that one did and the other
did not, or that they both did not. In this manuscript we will use the
word ‘hypothesis’ for such a possibility; another often used equivalent is
‘proposition’. In fact, we think it would be best to simply use the ter-
minology ‘explanation’ or ‘possibility’ to make clear that we investigate
which explanation is best, relative to the other ones that we consider. We
are exploring, using the evidential profile and the profiles of persons of
interest and taking the case context into account, how we can explain
the data that we have seen with various explanations that seem possible
and relevant within the case context. The word proposition suggests an
explanation that has been proposed by a party such as the prosecution or
the defense, but we believe that in exploring the possible explanations,
there should also be room for explanations that were not proposed by
either party, for example introduced by the ultimate finder of fact or by
the forensic laboratory. In the Discussion, we will go into this more
deeply as well as into the question of the assignment of (prior or pos-
terior) probabilities for the hypotheses. Since the terminology ‘hypoth-
esis’ is the one used by the Netherlands Forensic Institute, we adopt it in
this text as well. When there are multiple persons of interest, one may
compute several LR’s based on various pairs of hypotheses, for example

first logically extend the  approach behind Table 1  to  deal with an
arbitrary number of  contributors. We  illustrate this approach with
several examples inspired by cases encountered in actual casework. All
calculations were done with the package DNAxs ([6]) which contains for
LR calculations the software DNAStatistX, that uses the model of Euro-

DNAxs is proprietary software of the NFI, for which licenses can be
obtained; for the purposes of this article it is immaterial how the like-
lihoods were obtained, since we are concerned only with the subsequent
interpretation.
In Section 3 we discuss the problem of choosing prior probabilities
and how to report concisely and comprehensively at the same time. In
the Appendix we give a more formal derivation of the likelihood ratios
that we have discussed, and we consider computational obstacles that
can arise.
We note also that our approach is, in fact, inspired by the analogous
problem in kinship analysis. For a given set of individuals, there may be
various hypotheses corresponding to  pedigrees specifying how these
persons are related (or not). To determine which pedigree(s) are the best
candidate(s), one computes the  probability of  the  genetic evidence
under all hypotheses. Posterior probabilities can be obtained if there are
prior probabilities for all pedigrees. For example, the software Familias
([9]) can generate pedigrees, compute the likelihoods, and give poste-
rior probabilities for the generated pedigrees based on the genetic evi-
dence and prior probabilities for all pedigrees.

In our opinion, the question a fact finder has is what they should
believe regarding the  composition of  the  trace. This question is
answered by a table of likelihoods of all hypotheses in combination with
the fact finder’s priors. It is sometimes argued that every PoI has its own
trial and that therefore a  separate LR  per  PoI  should be  reported.
Without being a  legal expert in  any way, and only familiar with
inquisitorial justice systems such as the one in the Netherlands, we can
think of several arguments against this. First of all if one wants to leave
prior probabilities exclusively to the court, reporting LR’s per PoI is not
possible. Secondly, it is not really so that the PoI have their own trial. It
is more common to have all PoI tried in one trial, with the court first
reaching a conclusion as to what happened, who was involved in what,
then deciding on whether these acts are punishable offenses, and only in
the last stage reaching a verdict per PoI. It is not a sequential process
whereby first one person is tried, and the outcomes of that trial are used
to determine new facts to be used in the trial for the next PoI, etc. In
order for  the  court to  establish what has  happened, they are  best
informed if a laboratory reports the composition of the trace that is best
supported by the analysis. This will help them to arrive at a conclusion
about the course of events, a conclusion that needs to be drawn in order
for the court to draw the legal consequences for each PoI.
It may be that a single hypothesis stands out, such as in the example
in Section 2.1, but it may also be that this is not the case, such as in the
example in Section 2.3. In that example, there are two hypotheses, H

strong evidence for them to have both contributed, but that there is
nothing to be learned regarding PoI 3. In this case, one may argue that
reporting a LR per PoI would allow to convey that the evidence is much
stronger for PoI 1 than for PoI 2. But that this is so follows from the
likelihoods in Table 4, so one might as well report Table 4 instead. This
table is always sufficient, provided it contains all hypotheses that are
considered relevant.
Next, we discuss the choice of prior probabilities on the hypotheses.
As we saw, an assignment of a LR per PoI requires an assignment of such
prior probabilities. A logical question is: whose prior probabilities do we
mean here? There are several parties involved in the trial: a prosecution,
a defense, and a court; and then there is also the forensic laboratory.
Every party has its own prior probabilities, since these probabilities are
epistemic, reflecting one’s knowledge on the case and of the world (cf.
[11]). Parties can have different information and they may exchange
information, but they cannot tell other parties what priors to use. In
particular a  defendant cannot tell  the  fact finder to  put  their prior
probability entirely on H
uuu
, nor can the prosecution do so with H
## 123
## .
Defendants may know that they are innocent, or that they are guilty, or
perhaps may also not be sure about what happened exactly. But that is
not relevant here, nor is it relevant what the prosecution’s assessment is
of the prior probabilities (apart perhaps from that they need to be large
enough for the case to go to court in the first place), since these parties
do not make the verdict. It is the court that needs to decide, and hence
the court’s probabilities are what matter, and they should unbiased, i.e.,
based on  all  relevant information from both parties. Furthermore,
innocence is not equivalent to not being a DNA contributor. We are
concerned with hypotheses describing the source(s) of the DNA here. It
may be that an innocent reason exists for the DNA contribution of a PoI.
It may also be that a reason for not convicting exists even if the alleged
acts have taken place, such as self defense or insanity. Thus, it is also not
the case that if (say) PoI 1 is acquitted, one should by definition remove
PoI 1 from the hypotheses when the evidence is assessed for PoI 2. The
likelihoods of the hypotheses remain what they are, but from the context
it may be clear that some are less plausible than others a priori. That
judgment is, ultimately, for the court to make.
That is not to say a forensic lab can allow itself to comfortably retract

We have discussed how general conclusions and LR’s for specific PoI are most easily obtained from a table of likelihoods for all relevant hypotheses,
